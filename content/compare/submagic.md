## You didn't record a clip. You recorded a conversation.

That is usually the whole problem. There are 90 minutes of a podcast, a client session, or a founder call sitting on a drive, and what you need out of it is one finished episode and four or five verticals. Submagic starts one step after that. It takes a piece of footage and makes it look good — template captions, emoji, zooms, B-roll, a hook title. Its own help center describes the workflow plainly: upload, style, correct, export. For a clip under two minutes, that really is the fastest way to something you'd post.

The catch is how much you're allowed to bring in. Starter caps a video at 2 minutes, Pro at 5, Business at 30. Turning a long recording into shorts is Magic Clips, a separate $19 a month that hands you a batch of machine-picked highlights, 10 long videos a month.

BitterClip starts at the recording. Drop in the file — phone, Zoom, Riverside, a camera — and the transcript becomes the thing you edit. Find the tangent in the words, select it, delete it, and a real cut happens. The cut lands on the word rather than near it, because it is timed against the actual audio: no clipped syllables, no half-swallowed first word. One tap makes the vertical version, captions and timing carried across.

You can also skip the drop and record straight into the project. Every project has a browser recorder: Record in browser on the Sources tab, or Record here in the editor's insert sheet, which lands the finished take in the edit at the playhead. Prop your phone up and go — it takes camera and mic from a laptop or phone browser, or your screen in desktop Chrome, up to 1080p, and it uploads in short chunks while you record rather than leaving a big file on your device, so the take is transcribed by the time you press stop. Know the shape of it: one person, one device, signed in, with no remote guests and no guest recording links. For recording a guest over the internet, a dedicated remote-recording tool is still the better choice. Submagic doesn't record at all — it styles footage you already have.

## A template can read the text. It can't hear the point.

It doesn't know that "forty percent" is the number the whole clip exists to deliver, so the emphasis lands wherever the template's rules put it. Submagic gives you real tools to fix that — its guide walks through correcting transcript errors and moving word highlights and emoji — but the fixing is yours, caption by caption, clip by clip.

In BitterClip, captions are something you can ask for. Tell ChatGPT to highlight the price and the deadline and fix the founder's name everywhere it got misheard, and that is what happens: active-word highlight, accent color, placement, per-word corrections. Anything you can click, you can ask for — in ChatGPT, in Claude, or right in the editor.

## Fix the cut you have.

Credit where it's due: Submagic's output is editable, not take-it-or-leave-it. You can trim the timeline, correct the captions, change the hook, swap the B-roll. What its docs describe is a finishing pass over a result the machine already produced.

BitterClip is built the other way around. When the first cut isn't right, you say what's wrong and the same cut gets better — no starting over and hoping for a luckier draw. Undo works, all the way back. The review link you sent a client always plays the exact version they watched. And every clip remembers where it came from, so "where did this line actually come from" still has an answer months later.

The trade in one line: Submagic is a fast finish for footage you have already made the decisions about. BitterClip is where the deciding happens. The free plan gives you 10 exports a month with a watermark.
